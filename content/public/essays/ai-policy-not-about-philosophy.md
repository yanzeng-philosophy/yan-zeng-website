## 1. Starting From a Concrete Case

UCI's current AI policy is decentralized. The Graduate Division asks each graduate course to state its AI policy clearly in the syllabus, but the university does not impose a uniform rule about whether AI is allowed or prohibited. Each instructor draws the boundary within the scope of their own course.

That sounds reasonable. But it is worth setting it beside another, more concrete choice. In May 2026, the [University of Edinburgh's philosophy department](https://dailynous.com/2026/05/27/does-your-department-have-an-ai-policy-heres-edinburghs/) released an AI policy that attracted broad discussion. It prohibits students from using AI for writing, revising, or editing in all assessed work, and it explicitly includes ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and even Grammarly within the definition of "AI." The stated reason is that philosophy courses aim to cultivate careful original thought, develop persuasive academic writing, and deepen understanding of complex topics.

What matters is that this is a *default* policy. Individual instructors may override it with their own course policies. So the real difference between UCI and Edinburgh is not "decentralization vs. blanket prohibition." It is that the *direction of the default* is opposite. UCI has no default; Edinburgh's default is prohibition. And defaults are never neutral. They are sticky. Most instructors will not actively override them, so the default ends up shaping the actual world most students face. This will matter later.

At first glance, the policy looks like a strict ban on AI. But what is most striking is not its strictness. It is its justification structure.

It presupposes a very strong philosophical position:

> The production process itself is a constitutive part of philosophical formation.

Not just final judgment, not just the final conclusion, not just accountability, but writing, revising, editing, wording, and iterative prose construction. These activities themselves are treated as philosophically educational.

My first reaction to the policy was discomfort. But rather than saying "this rule is too strict," the more accurate description is this: **I began to wonder what philosophy departments are trying to protect when they make this kind of policy.**

## 2. Why Edinburgh's Position Is Not Stupid

To make a meaningful criticism, we first need to acknowledge the internal coherence of the policy. Behind it is a serious view of philosophical education: philosophy is not merely the production of a correct set of propositions, but a formation of intellectual character. A person's capacity to make high-quality philosophical judgments partly depends on having personally gone through certain experiences: struggling for a long time with a still-unformed thought, discovering an inconsistency they had not noticed through repeated prose revision, being forced by the wording of a sentence to clarify a concept. These experiences cannot be outsourced, because they help form the subject who later becomes capable of mature judgment.

This position has real philosophical resources behind it: virtue epistemology on intellectual character formation; Wittgenstein and Sellars on thinking-as-articulation, where thought is not fully formed first and then expressed, but carved out through articulation; Anscombe on practical knowledge.

If that is the policy's real position, then it is not simply an anti-technology conservatism. It is protecting a specific path of cognitive formation.

## 3. But the Question Has Been Misplaced

I used to think that responding to Edinburgh required answering the question, "What counts as constitutive of philosophical labor?" Later I realized that this way of responding carries a hidden premise: it assumes that philosophy, as a practice, has some essence, and that through careful analysis we can find the true boundary between its constitutive parts and its merely infrastructural parts.

But that premise may itself be wrong.

Philosophy as a practice has no fixed essence. It has a history, a community, and an internal trajectory of evolution. Edinburgh's policy is a present articulation, by one philosophical community, of what it means to do philosophy. It is not wrong because it misidentifies some essence, because there is no essence to misidentify. Likewise, those who disagree with it are not correct because they are closer to the true essence. All articulations are historical. They speak from within an evolving understanding of a practice.

If we stop here, the discussion threatens to slide into relativism: each community makes its own articulation, and no one can criticize anyone else. But that slide can be avoided.

The key distinction is this:

> Philosophy as a practice can lack a fixed purpose; education as an institutional arrangement must have a purpose, because its purpose is *chosen*.

Education is not a natural fact. It is a deliberately constructed arrangement. What kind of person it should cultivate is a normative question. We *choose* certain ends, such as "citizens with critical rational capacity," rather than *discover* them. This is selection, not discovery. And once any selection is made, fairness immediately becomes a *constitutive* issue. Once you choose "cultivating citizens with critical rational capacity" as the end, the question of *who can access that formation* becomes normatively unavoidable.

In other words, philosophy itself may suspend the question of purpose. But once philosophy enters education, it enters a domain with chosen ends, and therefore a domain that must be evaluated in terms of fairness. AI policy is not a policy about philosophy. It is a policy about education.

## 4. AI as a New Historical Variable

This changes how AI enters the discussion.

AI is not a threat to philosophy, nor is it philosophy's savior. AI is a new historical variable in the educational environment, like the expansion of print culture, the achievement of universal literacy, or the spread of search engines. Whenever variables of this kind appear, educational "environment engineering" has to respond reflectively.

A reflective response is not "accept every change." Nor is it "resist every change." It asks:

> Given our commitment to educational ends, how should this new variable be included or excluded so that those ends become more reachable?

That is the real question. It does not require us to answer "what is philosophy?" It requires us to acknowledge that we are committed to certain educational purposes, and that this commitment needs reflective articulation under new historical conditions.

Notably, Edinburgh's policy could be reformulated within this framework. They could say: "We, too, are committed to cultivating critical rational citizens, and *because of* that commitment, AI must be excluded. Without personally undergoing the struggle of prose construction, this capacity will not genuinely form."

That would be an honest disagreement. It moves the dispute away from "what is the essence of philosophy?" and toward "what kind of environment engineering reliably cultivates critical rational capacity?" That is a fight worth having.

But even on this new terrain, there is one step in Edinburgh's move that needs to be isolated.

"Cultivating intellectual character" is one thing. "This character must be formed through sustained prose iteration" is another. The policy treats these two claims as the same. It directly equates formation with struggling over prose. But this identification is both load-bearing and among the least examined assumptions in the policy.

One can fully grant that struggle with concepts is constitutive of formation while still asking: why must this struggle take the specific vehicle of *manual prose iteration*? A student may undergo real and difficult struggle in conceptual discrimination, inferential accountability, and normative positioning without having that struggle locked into the inherited vehicle of sentence-by-sentence prose revision. To equate formation with one specific vehicle is to treat a historically stabilized configuration as a necessity.

This point does not require proving that prose iteration has no value. It only requires pointing out that the *goal* of formation and one *historical vehicle* of formation are not the same thing, and that the policy collapses them.

## 5. Fairness as the Axis of Evaluation

Once the framework is changed in this way, fairness immediately becomes a first-order evaluative issue.

I have used the driving analogy in another essay, and I want to reuse it here, but not to talk about essence. I want to talk about access.

In the age of manual transmission, driving as access to mobility was closed to many people. It required time, resources, and coordination ability to learn the skill. Automatic transmission did not reveal the true essence of driving. It *redistributed access to mobility*. More people could drive; more people could access mobility. Preserving manual driving as a hobby or sport is perfectly fine. That is another practice. But it is not transportation policy.

The analogy to AI policy is this: blanket prohibition structurally over-represents students who are already fluent in academic writing, including native English speakers, students with implicit academic training, and students from academic family backgrounds. Thoughtful integration of AI is not a matter of "lowering standards." It is a matter of *redistributing access to philosophical formation*.

This does not mean that any form of integration is justified. Concrete policy design still has to answer many difficult questions. Which uses of AI actually promote the formation of critical capacity, and which ones short-circuit it? How granular should disclosure be? How should assessment be redesigned so that, after AI integration, it still evaluates reasoning rather than output? These are genuinely hard design problems.

But one thing is clear: once access rather than essence becomes the evaluative axis, the problem becomes tractable. We can ask, "Whom does this policy make it easier or harder for to form critical rational capacity?" rather than, "Does this policy protect some ineffable philosophical essence?"

## 6. So What Are Current Policies Actually Doing?

What we need here is a careful functional description, not an accusation about motivation.

I am not saying that Edinburgh's faculty are intentionally protecting the interests of their own group. I am saying this:

> Many current AI policies structurally function to preserve existing competence configurations, and those configurations are already closely correlated with the implicit training of certain social groups.

This is a structural critique, not an accusatory critique.

Moreover, this functional preservation is usually not the product of reflection. It is not that a community has carefully considered the matter and decided, "Yes, we intend to preserve this configuration." It is more like a default reaction. Faced with a new variable, a practice tends defensively to maintain its existing form, naturalizing the status quo as "what doing philosophy properly looks like."

There is a general worry here: once an educational arrangement begins defensively protecting its existing form, rather than reflectively engaging the conditions in which it is situated, what it protects is no longer the educational purpose but *a historically stabilized mode of production*. This worry applies to philosophy departments today, especially when a historical variable on the scale of AI is already changing the broader landscape of cognitive labor.

## 7. Conclusion: Change the Question

The real thesis of this essay is not "AI should be allowed." Nor is it "Edinburgh is wrong."

It is this:

> The current debate about AI policy is framed incorrectly. It pretends to be about "what philosophy is," but it is implicitly deciding "who education is open to."

Once we acknowledge that a practice has no fixed essence, that educational purposes are chosen, and that AI is a historical variable this generation of education must respond to reflectively, fairness immediately moves from a side concern to a first-order question.

If I have to take a position, then on this framing the balance tilts toward integration-with-transparency rather than blanket prohibition. Not because the essence of philosophy requires it, but because, given a chosen educational purpose and the fact that AI is now a standing condition, prohibition predictably concentrates access in the hands of those already fluent in academic writing. The concrete design of policy remains difficult, but the default should not be prohibition. It should be: how can the introduction of this variable produce greater equality of access?

This is not to say that the essence question does not exist. It is to say that it has been put in the wrong place. AI policy appears to ask "what is philosophy?" In practice, it decides "who is education open to?" The latter is what policy is really doing, and it is the standard by which policy should be evaluated.
