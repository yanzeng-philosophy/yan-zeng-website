## I. A scene that should be reassuring

I sit in California and write philosophy in Chinese. My AI tools translate fluently in both directions. My phone surfaces news in Chinese before it surfaces it in English. When I want to understand a paper in another language, a summary appears in seconds: friendly, idiomatic, already pre-digested. Nothing is forbidden to me. No firewall stands between me, here, and the rest of the world. By every formal measure, I am free.

But the habits I carry with me were formed inside an ecosystem where access, language, and platform had already been quietly arranged. The wall is not in front of me anymore; it was upstream. And what it built is still with me.

And yet something has changed. The English-language journal articles that once felt like home now feel slightly distant. Not difficult, exactly. Just farther. A small cost has appeared between me and them: the cost of attention, of slower reading, of words I have to look up because I have not used them in months. The cost feels trivial in any single moment, which is why I keep paying it. I keep choosing the path with less friction. And the path with less friction, more and more, runs through Chinese.

This essay is about what that drift means.

## II. The censorship we expect, and the kind we do not

When we picture threats to intellectual freedom, we picture walls. Banned books. Blocked websites. Officials at borders. The mental image is of someone trying to reach a piece of information and being stopped: visibly, identifiably stopped. The classic theory of liberty matches this picture: freedom is the absence of coercive interference. If no one is stopping you, you are free.

But there is a different, quieter form of control that this picture misses entirely. It works not by blocking access but by making the alternative so seamless, so well-translated, so emotionally satisfying, that you stop wanting access in the first place. The wall is not built. It is grown: out of recommendations, summaries, dubbed videos, AI tutors, native-language podcasts, mirror sites that arrive faster than the originals.

The result is not prohibition. It is **engineered dependence**: a state in which you remain formally free to seek the outside world but progressively less capable of doing so, because the capacity to do so has not been used and has begun to atrophy.

The danger of prohibition is that it provokes resistance. People notice the wall. They climb it.

The danger of engineered dependence is that it produces no resistance at all, because at every step, the person feels they are choosing.

It helps to distinguish three levels at which freedom can be limited. **Hard coercion** blocks you from accessing what you want. **Soft manipulation** nudges you toward some options and away from others. **Capacity erosion** does something different and stranger: it leaves your formal options intact while reshaping what you are still capable of choosing well. Most discussions of digital unfreedom focus on the first two. This essay is about the third: the one that has no censor, no algorithmic villain, and yet still produces a person who is freer in name than in fact.

The central danger, then, is not that I am prevented from reaching the outside world. It is that I become the kind of person for whom reaching it feels unnecessary, inefficient, exhausting. My formal freedom remains intact, but the capacities that make that freedom usable begin to decay. This is the soft enclosure: not a prison around the body, but an ecosystem around attention, language, and habit.

## III. Why this is harder to see

Three features make this form of unfreedom particularly slippery.

First, it is **locally rational**. Every choice to use the convenient option is reasonable on its own terms. Why read a nine-thousand-word paper in a second language when a competent summary exists in your native one? Why struggle through subtitled foreign news when domestic news is faster and clearer? The cost of friction is real; the benefit of avoiding it is real. Each individual decision is defensible. The trap is in the aggregate: thousands of locally rational choices add up to a globally diminished self.

Second, it is **invisible from inside**. The skills you no longer use do not announce their decay. You do not get a notification saying, "Your reading speed in English has dropped fifteen percent." You just notice, vaguely, that you read less. Or you do not notice at all. The worldview shaped by the dominant ecosystem does not feel like a worldview. It feels like reality.

Third, it is **comfortable**. Almost everything that erodes your independent capacity also makes you happier in the short term. Less effort. Less confusion. Less of that uncomfortable feeling of being slightly outside something, of not quite getting the joke. Most resistance to oppression draws on discomfort. Engineered dependence removes the discomfort first.

## IV. What is actually being lost

It is tempting to describe what is at stake as "information access," but that frames the issue too narrowly. Several distinct things are eroding at once.

**Epistemic access.** When you encounter the outside world only through translations, summaries, and domestically produced commentary, you stop receiving it directly. Even if nothing is falsified, everything is mediated. You learn what the foreign discourse is *about*, but you stop participating *in* it. A summary tells you the conclusion; reading the original lets you see the argument's pressure points, the things the author was uncertain about, the structure that makes some objections obvious and others invisible. The mediated version is almost always cleaner than the original, and the cleanness is part of the loss.

**Linguistic agency.** A second language is not a luxury. It is an infrastructure for participating in everything that happens in that language: legal documents, scholarly debates, professional opportunities, friendships, arguments, jokes. When the language slips, the infrastructure slips with it. You can technically still enter that world; you just enter as a slower, weaker version of yourself.

**Attentional shape.** Where your daily inputs come from, what you scroll, what your phone surfaces, what your friends share, slowly shapes what feels important, what feels topical, what feels like a real concern versus an abstract one. Your sense of "what is going on right now" is a function of which information ecosystem your attention lives in. Even your emotional rhythms, what makes you angry, what makes you laugh, start to follow the ecosystem's contours.

**Exit capacity.** Genuine freedom is not just being free to stay where you are; it is being able to leave. Real exit requires that the destination still be reachable when you decide to go. If your skills have decayed, your network has consolidated inside the ecosystem, and your professional habits depend on its tools, then leaving becomes prohibitively expensive even though no one is stopping you. The door is open; the legs are weak.

**Self-authorship.** Underneath all the others is the deepest loss. To be the author of your own beliefs, you need to be able to seek out, compare, verify, and reinterpret the world for yourself. When everything important is delivered to you pre-interpreted, that capacity goes unused. You stop forming your own route to reality. Eventually, you may not remember that there was ever a route to form.

## V. The asymmetry of decay

A crucial feature of this trap is that it is not symmetric. Acquiring a language, a skill, a habit of mind takes years. Losing it takes far less time. The same is true of intellectual access: maintaining a working relationship with a foreign discourse requires constant practice, while drifting out of it takes only a few months of consistently choosing the easier option.

This means that engineered dependence is, in practice, a one-way valve. You can slip into it without noticing. You cannot slip back out without deliberate, sustained effort: exactly the kind of effort the convenient ecosystem has trained you to avoid.

Markets understand this asymmetry intuitively, which is why "frictionless onboarding" is a goal and "frictionful exit" is a feature. The same logic, scaled up from a single product to an entire information ecosystem, becomes a quiet form of capture.

## VI. AI as an accelerant

The historical version of this dynamic existed long before AI. Empires translated foreign works to render them locally consumable; closed societies built parallel media to substitute for the foreign press; convenience-oriented platforms have always nudged users toward domestic content. None of this is new.

What is new is the speed and totality with which AI can build the parallel ecosystem. Translation that once required armies of human translators now happens in milliseconds. Summarization scales infinitely. Native-language explanations of any foreign idea can be generated on demand. The "domesticated copy" of the outside world used to lag the original by months or years, and was patchy, and was clearly secondhand. AI removes the lag, fills the gaps, and polishes the seams.

The effect is that the parallel ecosystem can now offer something close to a complete substitute for direct access: comprehensive, current, fluent, frictionless. Convenience has reached a level where it would be nearly irrational, in any single moment, to do the work of accessing the outside world directly. And so the work is not done.

This is not an argument against AI tools. I use them constantly, and I am better for it. It is an argument that a tool which removes friction also, by that very fact, removes the daily exercise that maintained a capacity. The cost is invisible because it is paid in capacities you do not notice losing.

## VII. When the wall builds the alternative

I have been writing about engineered dependence as if it were purely a matter of convenience, a soft enclosure that grows up around users when the local ecosystem becomes good enough on its own. But this leaves out something important about the case I know best.

In China, the convenience did not grow on its own. It was scaffolded by a wall. The Great Firewall is not separate from the lush domestic internet; it is partly responsible for it. By blocking Google, Facebook, YouTube, and Twitter, the firewall created a protected market in which Chinese platforms could grow without competition. Inside that protected space, an entire parallel ecosystem developed: search, social media, video, payments, AI tools, each of them comprehensive, deeply integrated with Chinese cultural life, built up over many years of accumulated content and network effects.

This produces a two-stage dynamic that the convenience-only story misses. The first stage is *prohibition*: a hard wall blocks foreign alternatives long enough for domestic ones to mature. The second stage is *substitution*: the domestic ecosystem becomes so complete that even if the wall came down tomorrow, most users would not bother going through it. The wall is the catalyst; dependence is the long-run equilibrium. From inside the second stage, only the convenience is visible. The wall, by then, is almost decorative.

It is worth being honest about what this also produces, because the picture is not entirely dark. Inside the protected space, an enormous amount of genuine cultural and intellectual work has happened. Chinese-language content across academic, technical, creative, and recreational domains exists in greater quantity and higher quality than it would in a counterfactual world where Chinese users had distributed their attention and labor across the global internet from the start. If the firewall had never been built, the anglophone internet would be larger and richer than it is, but the Chinese-language internet would be thinner. Cultural production is real; the Chinese-language ecosystem's vitality is not an illusion.

This is precisely what makes the trap so hard to dismantle. The dependence is not on something flimsy. It is on something good. Users have not been seduced by an inferior product; they have been habituated to a product that, in many domains, fits their lives better than the global alternative would. The convenience is real, the cultural fit is real, the network effects are real. The freedom that has been lost is also real, but it is being weighed, every day, against tangible benefits, and most of the time the benefits win on their own terms.

The pattern *first prohibition, then dependence* may be more general than the China case suggests. Any closed system that lasts long enough, if it is also productive, will tend to grow a sufficient ecosystem of its own. The structural lesson is that the soft enclosure and the hard wall are not competing models of unfreedom. They are sequential. The hard wall does the work the soft enclosure cannot do at the beginning, and the soft enclosure does the work the hard wall stops needing to do at the end.

## VIII. Freedom requires capacity and community

The standard picture of liberty focuses on the absence of interference. You are free if no one is preventing you from doing what you want. The phenomenon I have been describing suggests this picture is incomplete. Two further conditions matter at least as much.

The first is **capacity**. You must retain the abilities required to actually use the freedoms you formally have. A right to read foreign newspapers is hollow if you can no longer read them. A right to participate in international debates is hollow if you have no infrastructure to do so. A right to leave the platform is hollow if everyone you care about, and every habit you depend on, lives on it. Negative liberty without sustained capacity is a kind of legal fiction: a permission to walk through a door that has been moved to the top of a mountain. Capacities are maintained by use, and ecosystems that systematically remove the need for use are, in a real sense, capacity-eroding, even when they are also, and this matters, genuinely useful in the short term. The same convenience that helps me get more done in a day is, on a longer timescale, narrowing the range of things I am still capable of doing at all.

The second is **community**. Even individuals who carefully maintain their own capacities will find that those capacities are difficult to exercise alone. When everyone around you also drifts inside the same convenient ecosystem, your social and professional networks consolidate there too. The cost of leaving is no longer just your own decayed skills; it is the loss of the people you talk to, work with, share references with. Even if you, alone, retain the capacity to operate outside the ecosystem, doing so increasingly means doing so alone. A philosopher cut off from interlocutors is not exactly free. They are free in the way someone stranded on an island is free: nothing is stopping them, and nothing is reaching them either.

This makes engineered dependence a coordination problem, not just a matter of individual virtue. "I will keep reading in English; I will not let my skills slip" is not enough when the community your intellectual life depends on is the one being domesticated. The freedom in question is partly relational: it depends on having people to be free *with*. And it has no agent in the standard sense, no censor, no decision, no plan, only the cumulative weight of small reasonable choices. That, more than anything else, is why we struggle to name what is happening.

## IX. The friction we need

What can be done? I do not think the answer is to refuse useful tools, to romanticize struggle, or to cultivate friction for its own sake. That would be a different kind of foolishness. The answer, I think, is more modest and more strategic.

Treat some friction as a practice. Read the original sometimes, even when the summary would do. Spend time inside foreign-language environments not because you have to but because the muscle needs the work. Maintain at least one channel, a journal, a community, a regular reading habit, where the cost of access is paid in full and where the capacity stays alive. The point is not to avoid convenience. The point is to keep some part of your week deliberately inconvenient, the way an athlete keeps part of their week deliberately exhausting, because exhaustion is what builds the capacity that ease then gets to draw on.

And recognize that this is, at root, not a personal optimization problem. It is a question about what kind of self, and what kind of community, you want to remain capable of. The convenient ecosystem is not the enemy. The unattended drift is.

The walls we should worry about now are not the ones built to keep us out. They are the ones grown around us by our own thousand small choices: each one reasonable, each one comfortable, each one inviting us a little further inside.
