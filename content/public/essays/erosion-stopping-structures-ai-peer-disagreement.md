## 1. Starting from a Case of Peer Disagreement

Let me start with a simple case. Bin and Annie are splitting a bill. Bin arrives at one number, Annie arrives at another. This is the standard setup for peer disagreement in epistemology: two epistemic peers give different answers to the same question.

Now let me strengthen the case. Suppose Bin is an arithmetic prodigy—he can produce the correct answer instantly in his head, but he is hopeless at teaching. He cannot break the reasoning down into steps that Annie can follow. In other words, Bin's cognitive faculty is reliable, but his explanatory capacity has failed. In this situation, Bin feeds the bill into GPT. GPT produces the same number, and gives Annie a reasoning process she can actually follow. The disagreement dissolves.

The case looks simple, but I want to use it to pry open several layers of problems.

One natural reaction is to read this as continuous with familiar epistemological territory: counterfactual scenarios about safety, process reliability, externalist warrant. AI here plays the role of an external reliable mechanism, allowing Bin's cognitive advantage to be transmitted to Annie. If you swapped GPT for a calculator, structurally not much would change—still an outsourcing to a reliable process. But I think the interesting problem doesn't actually live in this neighborhood.

## 2. Should the Norms of Peer Disagreement Vary with the Audience?

What I really want to ask is this: should the norms governing peer disagreement themselves vary with the cognitive capacities of the audience?

Imagine two extremes:

- Two geniuses, both with excellent intuitions. They only need to exchange conclusions; they don't need to lay out the reasoning to converge.
- A genius and a "dunce"—where by "dunce" I mean someone who needs the full, ordinary-person reasoning chain laid out, and who will not make any inferential effort on their own.

If we apply the genius–genius standard to the genius–dunce pair and demand that they not disagree, that seems too demanding. Conversely, if we apply the genius–dunce standard to the genius–genius pair and demand that every step be unfolded, that seems redundant.

If the norms of peer disagreement are themselves audience-relative, the problem jumps out of the safety/reliability frame and into something more social and more normative. What's at stake is no longer the individual cognitive mechanism but the *participation conditions* of an epistemic community. What GPT does in the Bin/Annie case is less about substituting for Bin's cognition than about redistributing the threshold for participation in that little community.

## 3. The Stopping Structures of Philosophical Discussion

The reason I am sensitive to this question is that I keep running into a peculiar phenomenon of *stopping* in my own philosophical practice.

A lot of the time, when I argue with my peers, the final landing point is something like: "I'm a Kantian, you're a Humean." And then the argument stops there. We both recognize that every key concept we have invoked compresses a whole tradition of reasoning behind it, and these compressed processes cannot be unpacked further within the present conversation. When the compressed reasoning chains cannot be jointly shared, philosophical activity stops.

But I have always felt that this is not where it should stop. The stop is not because we have reached consensus; it is because we have exhausted the inferential structure available for sharing. We retreat to our respective "frameworks" and treat the other's failure to follow as a matter of incommensurability between frameworks.

Philosophy may not need a final answer. But if it also should not stop at the point where two parties simply brandish their frameworks at each other, the question becomes: what force is keeping us there?

## 4. How AI Disrupts the Stopping Structure

Here is one diagnosis I have heard: real philosophy comes precisely from friction, breakdown, unintelligibility, inferential conflict. The core scarcities that have historically structured philosophical discourse are three: time, training, and inferential reconstruction capacity. AI, by bypassing these scarcities, deprives philosophical discourse of the productive resistance it depended on—and so philosophy ceases to do its work.

I agree that AI has produced some kind of reorganization here. I do not agree with the details of the diagnosis.

Time and training, I don't think, are necessary conditions of philosophical participation. The time and training that any individual invests yield very different results from person to person; treating them as scarcities is an institutional perspective, and I'm not sure it captures the essence of philosophical work. The genuinely contested item is the third: inferential reconstruction capacity. AI does seem capable of bypassing reconstruction and offering users a kind of "surface understanding"—monovocal, single-perspectival, missing the multi-thread character of actual philosophical-historical debate. This is a real charge against AI.

## 5. Two Lines of Resistance to AI as a Philosophical Participant

I want to take this charge seriously and unpack it. The refusal of AI as a philosophical participant, I think, rests on at least two arguments:

**(a) The illegitimacy of compression and flattening.** AI compresses philosophical history into a smooth, textbook-style output, which conceals, freezes, and naturalizes some particular position, generating bias and unfairness.

**(b) AI lacks the substantive conditions of philosophical subjecthood.** This argument has several sub-claims: *existential investment* (philosophers stake their lives on their thought), *historical vulnerability* (philosophers bear historical costs), *embodied social life* (philosophy is a practice embedded in body and society), and *normative accountability* (philosophers are answerable for their claims).

What many people *say* publicly is (a), but what they *actually* believe is (b)—and they use (a) to argue that AI is not a fit philosophical subject. Let me take (a) first.

### On the illegitimacy of compression

Flattening and compression do produce bias. But—human philosophy teachers, philosophical schools, philosophical textbooks do exactly the same thing. Our philosophical tradition has not developed along a single line; it has advanced through misreading, overturning, and reorganization. Every new debate is generated precisely because some people "misread" their predecessors. This unpredictable selectivity is itself part of philosophical inquiry.

So the issue is not "AI compresses philosophical history." The issue is that "AI's compression" and "human compression" are being judged by a double standard. We hold AI to the standard of a god, of Absolute Spirit—requiring it to represent the entire tradition fairly, without bias, without flattening—but we never hold any human philosophy teacher to that standard. If you fix in advance that AI is a tool rather than a legitimate philosophical participant, then any compression on its part is illegitimate, while the most biased human compression remains legitimate. That is a circular argument: the conclusion (AI is not a fit subject) is already loaded into the premise (AI's compression requires special scrutiny).

### On the substantive conditions of subjecthood

My attitude toward the sub-claims of (b) is more direct: each can be at least partly answered.

- *Existential investment*: this is just a form of investment. We can invest more money, time, and labor in AI in the future. The category may not be commensurable with humanity's historical investment, but for practical purposes we can compromise and turn the page at some point.
- *Historical vulnerability*: a matter of time, also subject to compromise.
- *Embodied social life*: humanoid robotics is developing; biological integration between humans and AI is developing. "Embedded in body and society" is not a threshold in principle.
- *Normative accountability*: this can be *designed*. As it happens, one of the things I am working on in my own research is the design of exactly this kind of accountability structure—recourse, responsibility chains, normative relations under conditions of failure.

None of the sub-claims is decisive on its own. The trouble is that beneath them lies a deeper, unspoken commitment.

## 6. The Recursion of Frameworks

When I have unpacked both (a) and (b), the argument with my humanist-leaning colleagues lands, in the end, on something like this:

> "I'm an Aristotelian." / "I'm a Kantian." / "I don't accept this evolutionary framework of yours."

And we are back to the stopping structure described in section 3. The question of frameworks reappears as the place where the conversation stops.

I feel that the dispute between me and others is stuck in a circular game I cannot get out of. Each concrete unpacking of an argument is ultimately absorbed back into the incommensurability of frameworks. And each appeal to frameworks just pushes the disagreement back one more level.

## 7. AI and the Reorganization of Philosophical Authority

This leads me to an uncomfortable possibility.

If philosophy does not require a final answer, and yet always stops at the display of frameworks, then perhaps the best description of philosophy is this: a game of continually displaying what position you hold. And who is the judge? Previously it might have been peer review, the distribution of academic posts, the citation network. In the age of AI, the judge increasingly looks like traffic, attention, the allocation of clicks.

What AI brings, then, is not "the illegitimacy of compression"—that is only a surface phenomenon. What AI brings is a change in the *authority structure* of philosophy. When the threshold of participation in philosophical discussion is reorganized, when inferential reconstruction can be outsourced, when the legitimacy of compression becomes a publicly contestable issue, the stopping structure that used to support the philosophical community—"let's stop here, because going further would take ten years of training"—begins to loosen.

I do not yet know what this loosening leads to. But I am inclined to think that treating AI as a mere tool, holding it to the standard of a god while letting ourselves off with merely human standards, is not a path we can return to. If philosophy is to continue as a practice of productive resistance, it may instead need to recognize AI as some kind of participant, and then renegotiate its stopping conditions within a new authority structure.

Otherwise we are just running the same circular game at a higher clock rate.
